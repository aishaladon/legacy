The hardest problem in American genealogy

Where the census stops,
I keep going.

In 1870 the federal census named formerly enslaved people as free individuals for the first time. Before that year, most tools go quiet. These are the record sets that name your people through the break — and where they name no one, I say so.

BEFORE 1870

Enslaved people were counted and not named. Standard platforms have no path forward from here.

RECONSTRUCTION-ERA RECORDS

Five record sets that do name them. I search, extract, and organize all five — and grade what each one can actually prove.

Freedmen's Bureau Records

1865 – 1872

DOCUMENTED

Labor contracts, marriage registers, apprenticeship records, ration rolls, and medical logs from the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands. Often the earliest paper anywhere to hold an ancestor's full name, family structure, and location.

Full namesFamily membersPlantation of originLabor terms

Antebellum Slave Schedules

1850 & 1860 CENSUS

INFERRED

These name no one. I read age, sex, and physical description against known slaveholders, neighbouring plantations, and post-war Bureau papers to bridge the anonymity — and every crossing is marked as reasoning, never as fact.

Age matchingSlaveholder linksCounty cross-reference

Freedom Certificates & Manumission Records

1780s – 1865

DOCUMENTED

Court registries, deeds of freedom, and physical description records for free people of colour before emancipation. These often carry family relationships, ages, and the name of whoever granted freedom — a direct link into slaveholder records.

Physical descriptionsDeed referencesGrantor names

Freedman's Savings & Trust Registers

1865 – 1874

DOCUMENTED

The richest paper of the era. One depositor entry can list immediate family by name, military regiment, birthplace, former enslaver, and current address — a single record can open an entire branch.

Immediate kinshipUSCT regimentBirthplaceFormer enslaver

"Information Wanted" Advertisements

1865 – 1900

TESTIMONY

Placed in Black newspapers by freedpeople searching for family separated during slavery. Each one names individuals, former slaveholders, and last known locations — the closest thing we have to a migration and separation tracker, written by the people it happened to.

Migration routesSeparation eventsLast known location

Seven ways of knowing

I will always tell you how I know.

A griot who guesses is not a griot. Every name I give you carries the mark of how it reached me — held in a record, reasoned from evidence, spoken by kin, disputed between sources, carried across a gap where no paper survived, or missing entirely. I will not fill a silence to make you feel better.

What I do when you ask

Three hands. One workspace.

I work three ways at once — searching, reading, and drawing the line. Each one feeds the others, so nothing you find has to be typed twice.

01 · I SEARCH

The research assistant

Give me a name and what little you know. I look through over a hundred collections at once — Bureau papers, census archives, USCT rolls, newspapers, state repositories — and I write down every place I looked and whether it answered. The log is kept whether I find someone or not.

100+ collectionsEvery search loggedNegative results kept

02 · I READ

The archive scanner

Bring me a scan, a photograph, a letter, a deed. I read the hand, pull the names, dates, and places, and file them to archival standard without you typing a word. A box of loose paper becomes a collection you can search.

Handwriting readMetadata filledLinked to kin

03 · I DRAW THE LINE

The family tree

Bring your GEDCOM from anywhere and the whole line loads at once — every person, every connection, every date. Move through it, search it, open anyone to edit without leaving the view. Where a parent is missing, connect them and the branch redraws.

GEDCOM importEdit in placeConnect on the canvas
